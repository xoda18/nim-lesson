The game from Marienbad

In a strange old hotel, a man plays a simple game with matches against the other guests. He wins every time.

This is a scene from the French film Last Year at Marienbad (1961). The rules are simple. 16 matches lie in four rows of 1, 3, 5 and 7. Two players take turns. On your turn you take as many matches as you like (at least one), but only from one row. The player who takes the last match wins.

(In the film, the player who takes the last match loses. We play the more common version. The idea is almost the same.)

The man always lets his opponent start. Try it yourself below. You go first.

The game is very old and is called Nim. In 1901 Charles Bouton, a mathematician at Harvard, worked out a complete winning strategy. In the 1930s Roland Sprague in Germany and Patrick Grundy in England, working independently, proved something much stronger: every game of this kind is Nim in disguise.

By the end of this lesson you will know what that means, and why the man in the hotel never loses.

      Many rows: Bouton's trick

      Back to many rows, where you may take any number of matches from one row. With several rows the pattern is much harder to guess, so here is the trick Bouton found.

      Step 1. Split every row into powers of two: 1, 2, 4, 8, … Every whole number can be written as a sum of different powers of two in exactly one way. For example, 7 = 4+2+1, 5 = 4+1, 3 = 2+1, 6 = 4+2.

      Step 2. Look at all rows together and count how many times each power appears. If every power appears an even number of times, we call the position balanced. If not, it is unbalanced.

      Here is the game from Marienbad, 1-3-5-7:

      row421
      11
      321
      541
      7421
      count224

      All the counts are even, so 1-3-5-7 is balanced.

      Bouton's theorem. If the position is balanced, the player to move loses, as long as the opponent plays well. If it is unbalanced, the player to move can always make it balanced, and win.

      Why does this work? Two facts:

      • Any move from a balanced position makes it unbalanced. You change only one row, so at least one power changes its count by one, and an even count becomes odd.
      • From an unbalanced position there is always a move that makes it balanced.

      The end of the game, when no matches are left, is balanced. So if you always hand your opponent a balanced position, they can never take the last match, and you will be the one who takes it.

      This is the secret from Marienbad: 1-3-5-7 is balanced, which is why the man lets his opponent start.

      A note on words: mathematicians call this counting the "nim-sum", and programmers know it as XOR. You won't need either word in this lesson. More on Nim on Wikipedia.

        Every game is Nim in disguise

        Take the game from page 2 again: one row, take 1, 2 or 3. It does not look like Nim. Still, we can give every position a number, its Nim value, which tells us which Nim row it behaves like.

        The rule: a position with no moves has value 0. For any other position, list the values of all the positions you can move to. The Nim value is the smallest number (0, 1, 2, …) that is not on the list.

        For "take 1, 2 or 3":

        • 0 matches: no moves, so the value is 0.
        • 1 match: you can go to 0 (value 0). The smallest missing number is 1.
        • 2 matches: you can go to 1 or 0, with values 1 and 0. The value is 2.
        • 3 matches: the values you can reach are 2, 1 and 0. The value is 3.
        • 4 matches: you can go to 3, 2 or 1, with values 3, 2 and 1. 0 is missing, so the value is 0.

        Look where the 0s are: at 0, 4, 8 and 12. These are exactly the losing positions from page 2.

        A Nim row of n matches has Nim value n, because from it you can move to every smaller number. Sprague and Grundy proved that this works for every game of this kind:

        The Sprague-Grundy theorem. A position with Nim value v behaves exactly like a Nim row of v matches.

        So a row of 6 in the "take 1, 2 or 3" game acts just like a Nim row of 2. When several games are played side by side (on your turn you move in one of them), you replace each game by its Nim value and use Bouton's trick on those values.

        Sprague published this in 1935 and Grundy in 1939, without knowing about each other's work. Decades later John Conway, Elwyn Berlekamp and Richard Guy built a whole theory of games on top of it in their book Winning Ways for your Mathematical Plays (1982). More on the Sprague-Grundy theorem on Wikipedia.

            Where this shows up

            AlphaZero is the program that beat the best players in the world at chess and Go. It has real trouble with Nim. In 2022 Bei Zhou and Søren Riis trained programs like it on Nim and found that they play well on small boards but can't keep up as the boards get bigger. The winning rule depends on whether counts are even or odd, which is exactly what you did with powers of two, and neural networks are bad at learning that kind of rule. (the paper)

            In 1951 the British company Ferranti built Nimrod, one of the first computers made to play a game. It could only play Nim. It drew crowds at the Festival of Britain and then at an industry fair in Berlin. In Berlin the West German economics minister, Ludwig Erhard, played it three times and lost every game. (Nimrod on Wikipedia)

            Error-correcting codes let you send data so that it arrives correctly even if a few bits get damaged on the way. In 1986 John Conway and Neil Sloane found that some of the best known codes come out of games: list the losing positions of the right game and you get the code. One of them is the Golay code. Around 1980, Voyager 1 and 2 used it to send color pictures of Jupiter and Saturn back to Earth. The code was invented in 1949, long before anyone thought it had anything to do with games. (lexicographic codes)
